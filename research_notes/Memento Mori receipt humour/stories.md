# Historical and artistic Memento Mori inspiration for receipt humour

Research date: 2026-11-25. Scope: institutional collections and primary author texts; original receipt lines below are creative proposals, not historical quotations. This is a researched selection, not a claim to have found every joke online. No product/code changes made.

## Which stories and motifs are defensible?

### Takeaway

The strongest varied mechanisms are equal treatment at the final checkout, possessions that cannot accompany their owner, expiring beauty, unrefillable time, disrupted routines, and the body as an out-of-print edition. The research supports these as cultural motifs; whether a new joke is funny is an editorial judgement.

### Cited findings: 14 useful source URLs

1. **Vanitas as an everyday reminder.** The Museum of Sepulchral Culture explains that vanitas pictures in private homes reminded viewers of mortality. Its still life links a consumed/extinguished candle with passing life, soap bubbles with fleeting existence, and lists wilting flowers, sand clocks, fading music, overturned glasses and books among related motifs. Useful for a varied symbolic inventory rather than multiple equivalent countdowns. [Museum of Sepulchral Culture](https://www.sepulkralmuseum.de/en/sammlungen/einblicke-in-die-sammlung/vanitas-stillleben/).

2. **Mortality can coexist with irony.** The Andy Warhol Museum explicitly treats vanity, mortality, time, irony and humour together, naming skulls, extinguished candles, wilting flowers, bubbles and timepieces as vanitas symbols. This supports a playful contemporary treatment without pretending the jokes themselves are old. [Andy Warhol: Vanitas](https://www.warhol.org/exhibition/vanitas/).

3. **The skull that appears when you change viewpoint.** Holbein's *The Ambassadors* includes a distorted skull that resolves from an oblique angle. The National Gallery explains its mortality reminder amid wealth and learning. Excellent basis for a perspective gag. Do not turn every instrument in the painting into a death symbol: the broken lute string also has discord-related interpretation. [National Gallery](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/hans-holbein-the-younger-the-ambassadors).

4. **A skull held by a young person.** Hals's painting is catalogued as *Young Man holding a Skull (Vanitas)*. The collection record is useful visual corroboration for the skull motif; it is not evidence for a specific witty phrase or the sitter's identity as Hamlet. [National Gallery](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/frans-hals-young-man-holding-a-skull-vanitas).

5. **Death ignores rank.** The Fisher Library explains the Dance of Death as a dialogue/procession involving people across social ranks. It traces Holbein's designs and Hollar's later etchings. Receipt translation: no priority lane, no status exemption, no premium account extension. [University of Toronto, Wenceslaus Hollar collection](https://collections.library.utoronto.ca/explore/hollar/books/dancedeath).

6. **The cemetery procession.** Bridwell Library describes early Dance of Death wall paintings and the ordered procession of religious and secular ranks. This is evidence for the shared social levelling motif, not proof of a single universal medieval version. [SMU, Dance of Death before Holbein](https://bridwell.omeka.net/exhibits/show/danceofdeath/beforeholbein).

7. **The actual printed objects.** The Met collection records Holbein/Lützelburger's *Dance of Death*, with woodcuts made around 1526 and publication in 1538. Use as an object-level reference for artwork provenance; the interpretive argument comes from the libraries above. [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/360011).

8. **Death interrupts ordinary life.** The Morgan's exhibition describes Holbein and Lützelburger developing the medieval procession into individual scenes where Death interrupts daily activity. This is a useful structure for modern receipt jokes about unfinished business. Access limitation: the search-index exhibition description was available, but a direct open returned 403. [Morgan Library, Images of Death](https://www.themorgan.org/exhibitions/online/holbein/images-of-death).

9. **Roman triumph anecdote, with a reliability warning.** Tertullian's *Apology*, chapter 33, says a person behind the triumphing ruler reminded him that he was human. The relevant transmitted wording concerns remembering one's humanity. It does not itself establish the popular modern package of a slave continuously whispering the exact words “memento mori” at every Roman triumph. Cite this as Tertullian's report, not a securely established universal ceremony or an attested Caesar quotation. [Tertullian, Apology, Reeve translation](https://www.tertullian.org/articles/reeve_apology.htm).

10. **Stoicism: present choices rather than death statistics.** In *Meditations* II, Marcus Aurelius considers the possibility of death as reason to govern present actions; he also distinguishes the present one has from past/future one does not possess. This supports urgency and attention jokes, not personalised lifespan claims. Use the primary text in George Long's translation rather than unaudited modern quote cards. [MIT Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.2.two.html).

11. **Time accounting is already a literary mechanism.** Seneca's *On the Shortness of Life*, in Aubrey Stewart's translation, contrasts time well used with busyness and regrets. His financial language about how life is spent makes receipt/account metaphors especially appropriate. He is making an ethical argument, not estimating any customer's remaining years. [Project Gutenberg, Minor Dialogues](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/64576/64576-h/64576-h.htm).

12. **A deathbed instruction manual.** Bridwell's *Ars Moriendi* exhibit describes a late-medieval Christian genre that prepared believers for dying, with eleven woodcuts and advice concerning faith, confession, consolation and attachment to possessions/status. One negative example depicts a dying man kicking away helpers. Safe editorial mechanism: the absurdity of leaving the instruction manual unread; do not present the religious doctrine as a universal contemporary truth. [SMU, Ars Moriendi](https://bridwell.omeka.net/exhibits/show/worshipdevotion/arsmoriendi).

13. **Deathbed attachment to money.** The National Gallery of Art explains Bosch's *Death and the Miser* through the deathbed imagery of *Ars Moriendi*. It supplies a concrete artwork for the conflict between worldly attachment and the final moment. It does not support apocryphal celebrity last words. [National Gallery of Art](https://www.nga.gov/artworks/41645-death-and-miser).

14. **Franklin's body as a book.** The Library of Congress identifies Franklin's humorous 1728 proposed epitaph and notes that its displayed manuscript is not in his own hand. The verse compares the printer's body to a book whose contents are gone and imagines a corrected new edition; the reproduced primary text can be consulted in his memoirs. Treat this as his proposed literary epitaph, not necessarily his actual gravestone inscription. [Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/franklin/franklin-epitaph.html); [primary text reproduction, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/36338/36338-h/36338-h.htm).

### Inferences

- The receipt can function like a pocket vanitas: one glance at everyday administrative language makes mortality feel unexpectedly concrete. This is our design inference, not a museum's claim about this product.
- One numeric time line plus several different symbolic mechanisms is likely stronger than three relabelled weekly counts. Humour quality needs human review.
- The best historical connections need no quotation on the product. Original phrasing avoids unattributed quotation problems and makes room for the user's own tone.

### Gaps

- No exhaustive survey can establish “all” Memento Mori humour; this selection focuses on defensible, reusable motifs.
- No last-words anecdote was reliable enough to recommend here. Famous deathbed one-liners should be individually traced before inclusion.
- The Morgan direct page was inaccessible; the search-index description is a lower-confidence access path than the opened institutional pages.
- Franklin's LOC transcript link timed out; the LOC exhibit confirms authorship/context and Gutenberg reproduces the verse.

## Which new receipt lines could use these themes?

### Takeaway

All 20 label/value pairs below are **ORIGINAL CREATIVE PROPOSALS written for this task**, not quotations collected from historical sources. Historical inspiration is noted to keep provenance honest. None claims to calculate a death date or future funerals.

### Original proposals

1. **EGO CHECK — INCLUDED IN PRICE** (Roman humility anecdote; source 9).
2. **VIP EXIT — SAME DOOR** (Dance of Death, sources 5–6).
3. **PREMIUM MEMBERSHIP — STILL MORTAL** (social levelling, sources 5–6).
4. **FINAL DANCE — PARTNER ASSIGNED** (Dance of Death, source 5).
5. **WEALTH AT DEPARTURE — HAND LUGGAGE: NONE** (vanitas / miser, sources 1 and 13).
6. **PERSONAL BAGGAGE — LEFT TO THE FAMILY** (worldly attachment, sources 12–13).
7. **CANDLE WARRANTY — VOID WHEN LIT** (vanitas candle, source 1).
8. **HOURGLASS REFILL — OUT OF STOCK** (sand clock, source 1).
9. **BEAUTY SUBSCRIPTION — CANCELS ITSELF** (wilting flowers, sources 1–2).
10. **BUBBLE PROTECTION — NOT INCLUDED** (soap-bubble fragility, sources 1–2).
11. **FINAL NOTE — NO ENCORE GUARANTEED** (fading music, source 1).
12. **SKULL — FACTORY INSTALLED** (skull motif, sources 1 and 4).
13. **CHANGE OF PERSPECTIVE — SKULL INCLUDED** (Holbein anamorphosis, source 3).
14. **BODY COPY — GOING OUT OF PRINT** (Franklin's book/body metaphor, source 14).
15. **NEXT EDITION — ASK THE AUTHOR** (Franklin's revision metaphor, source 14).
16. **TIME SPENT WORRYING — NONREFUNDABLE** (Stoic attention, sources 10–11).
17. **TOMORROW'S CREDIT — NOT APPROVED** (future uncertainty, sources 10–11).
18. **EXIT INSTRUCTIONS — STILL IN THE BOX** (Ars Moriendi manual, source 12).
19. **UNFINISHED BUSINESS — ACCEPTED AS IS** (Death interrupts routines, source 8).
20. **HEIRLOOM VALUE — SOMEONE ELSE'S PROBLEM** (possessions/status vanitas, sources 1 and 13).

### Inferences and editorial recommendation

For one short receipt, choose at most five or six of these. The strongest compact set is SKULL / FACTORY INSTALLED; HOURGLASS REFILL / OUT OF STOCK; VIP EXIT / SAME DOOR; TIME SPENT WORRYING / NONREFUNDABLE; UNFINISHED BUSINESS / ACCEPTED AS IS. They vary object joke, retail joke, status reversal and philosophical punchline. Keep the required footer exactly: **Thanks for living, come again!**

### Gaps

These drafts are not checked for pre-existing coincidental similar jokes or trademarks. They are original compositions in this research session, not a promise that nobody has ever independently used a similar short phrase. Long label/value combinations need layout testing before use on a 4 × 16-inch print.
